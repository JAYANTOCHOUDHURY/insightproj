Movement Over Cuts — The Case for Long Takes

Nutan Kumar Founder & Executive Producer
November 20, 2025 · 7 min read

When done right, a long take can say more than fifty cuts. We break down our favourite ones — and why they work.

Editing is the language of cinema. That's the standard story. The cut is the fundamental unit, the comma and full stop of visual storytelling. Without it, we'd just be watching theatre.

But there's a counterargument that's just as true: some of the most powerful moments in film history happen when the cut doesn't come. When the camera stays with a character. When time unfolds without interruption. When the audience is forced to sit in a single, continuous moment.

That's the long take. And when it's done well, it's unforgettable.

Why Long Takes Are Hard

Let's get the obvious out of the way: long takes are difficult. Everything has to work in real time — the acting, the lighting, the focus, the camera movement, the blocking, the sound. There's no safety net of another take to cut away to. If one element fails, the whole take fails.

That's why most productions avoid them. It's easier to shoot in fragments and assemble in the edit. Coverage is safe. Coverage is forgiving. Coverage is how you finish a film on schedule.

But coverage also creates a certain kind of film — one that's assembled rather than experienced. The long take trades safety for something else entirely.

What Long Takes Give You

When you hold a shot, you change the relationship between the audience and the story.

1. Real Time

A cut can cover five minutes of story in five seconds. A long take can't. It forces the film to experience time the way the audience does — one second at a time. This is why long takes feel so immediate. There's no editing trickery to soften the blow.

2. Continuous Space

When we cut, we're always reminding the audience that they're watching a film. When we don't cut, we give them something closer to reality — an uninterrupted view of a world that continues even when the camera turns away.

3. Witness

Long takes make the audience a witness. Not a spectator who can look away when things get uncomfortable, but someone who has to stay in the moment. This is why long takes are so effective in scenes of tension, grief, or violence — they deny the relief of a cut.

4. Performance

Long takes give actors room to build. Instead of performing a single emotional beat in a five-second clip, they get to carry a moment through its full arc. Some of the most extraordinary performances in film history exist inside single unbroken takes.

The Masters of the Long Take

There are filmmakers whose entire style is built around the long take. Here are a few worth studying.

Alfonso Cuarón — Children of Men and Gravity are masterclasses in long-take action. The famous car ambush sequence in Children of Men is a single, unbroken shot that feels more intense than any edited sequence could.

Alejandro González Iñárritu — Birdman is famously constructed to look like one continuous take. The illusion is the point: the film traps the audience inside its protagonist's fractured mind.

Andrei Tarkovsky — His long takes are slow, patient, and almost meditative. They're not about action. They're about presence. His films teach you to sit with an image until it becomes something else.

Béla Tarr — Tarr's films often consist of fewer than thirty shots total. His long takes aren't just technique — they're philosophy. Time is the subject.

Sam Mendes — 1917 is built around the illusion of one continuous take. The technique forces the audience to walk through the war alongside the characters, with no relief.

When Not to Use a Long Take

The long take is not always the right choice. Used badly, it becomes a party trick — a director showing off rather than serving the story.

Here's when we avoid them at Cloudsartist21:

When the scene is dialogue-heavy. Long takes work best when there's something physical happening. A conversation held for five minutes in a single shot often drags.

When the story needs acceleration. If the film is building momentum, cutting is often more effective. A long take slows everything down.

When the shot doesn't add meaning. If you can achieve the same emotional effect with coverage, do that instead. Long takes should always earn their length.

How We Approach Them

When we decide to build a scene around a long take, we plan obsessively. Every beat is choreographed. The camera movement is rehearsed until it becomes muscle memory. We do table reads, then blocking rehearsals, then camera rehearsals — often for days before we roll.

On the day, we shoot more takes than usual. Even if the first one is perfect, we shoot it again. And again. Because with a long take, "good enough" isn't good enough. It has to be flawless.

The result is a shot that feels effortless to the audience. But the effort is enormous. That's the trade-off. The audience gets magic. The crew gets exhaustion.

Final Thoughts

Editing and long takes aren't opposites. They're two tools in the same toolbox. The best filmmakers know when to cut and when to hold. When to break time and when to let it flow.

The long take isn't better than the cut. It's different. It achieves something the cut can't — presence, witness, uninterrupted time. And when a film needs that, there's no substitute.
