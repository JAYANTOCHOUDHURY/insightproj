What Goes Into a 30-Second Commercial

Nutan Kumar Founder & Executive Producer
November 12, 2025 · 5 min read

Thirty seconds on screen. Six weeks of work off screen. A look at the real production timeline of a brand TVC.

When you watch a 30-second commercial, it feels effortless. The story is clear, the visuals are polished, the music swells at exactly the right moment. It looks like something that could have been made in an afternoon.

It wasn't. A 30-second spot is one of the most compressed forms of filmmaking there is. Every second on screen represents hours of planning, shooting, and post-production. Here's what that actually looks like.

The Brief (Week 0)

Every commercial begins with a conversation. The client comes with a product, a target audience, and a message they need to land. Our job is to translate that into a story that can be told in 30 seconds.

This stage is all about asking the right questions. Who is this for? What do they care about? What emotion should they feel when the logo appears? The answers shape everything that follows.

Concept & Script (Week 1)

Once the brief is clear, we develop concepts. Usually three. Each one takes a different angle on the same message — some literal, some metaphorical, some emotional.

The client picks one (or asks for a hybrid). From there, we write the script. A 30-second script is roughly 60–80 words of dialogue, plus detailed action lines. Every word matters. Every pause matters. A single extra sentence can throw off the entire rhythm.

Pre-Production (Week 2–3)

This is the longest and most invisible part of the process. It's also where the commercial is really made.

Casting. We audition actors, review portfolios, and run callbacks. The right face can carry an entire story without a word of dialogue.

Location scouting. Whether it's a studio, a home, a street, or a resort, the location has to serve the story. We shoot test footage, check light angles, and plan the camera movement in advance.

Shot listing and storyboards. Every frame of a 30-second commercial is planned. We know exactly how many setups we need, in what order, and how long each will take. There's no room for improvisation on the day.

Wardrobe, props, and production design. All the details that make a world feel real — chosen, sourced, and prepared in advance.

The Shoot (Week 4)

A typical 30-second commercial is shot in one or two days. That sounds fast, but it's intense. A 12-hour shoot day can produce anywhere from 15 to 30 setups, depending on complexity.

On set, the director, DP, AD, gaffer, sound recordist, and production team all work in sync. There's no time for debates. Decisions are made quickly, and the camera keeps rolling.

The most important rule on a commercial shoot: get the shot. If the light is fading, shoot. If the actor is nailing the take, shoot. If the schedule is slipping, adapt and shoot. Everything else is secondary.

Post-Production (Week 5–6)

Once the footage is in, the real work begins.

Editing. The editor assembles a rough cut, then a fine cut, then a final cut. Each pass tightens the story, removes anything unnecessary, and finds the rhythm. A commercial is often cut down from 4–5 minutes of selects to 30 seconds.

Sound design and music. A commercial's soundscape is as important as its visuals. Music, effects, and dialogue are all mixed to support the emotion of the story.

Color grading. This is where the film gets its final look — warm, cool, cinematic, natural. A well-graded commercial feels premium even when it's selling something simple.

Client feedback and revisions. The final cut goes to the client, who may request changes. Good production houses build in time for two to three rounds of revisions.

Delivery (Week 6–7)

Once the commercial is approved, we deliver it in multiple formats — broadcast, digital, social media, and any other platform the client needs. Each version may need its own aspect ratio, duration, and technical specs.

The project is done. The commercial goes live. And for thirty seconds, an audience somewhere will experience something that took six weeks to build.

Why This Matters

Understanding the production timeline changes how you watch commercials. You start noticing the details — the light, the cut, the music cue, the small performance beats. You realise that nothing on screen is accidental.

It also changes how you produce them. When you know that 30 seconds costs weeks of work, you stop underestimating the craft. You respect the process. And you make better films.

Final Thoughts

A 30-second commercial is a compressed story. It has a beginning, middle, and end. It has characters, conflict, and resolution. It has a point of view.

It just has less time to say it. That's what makes it such a fascinating form — and why we love making them.
